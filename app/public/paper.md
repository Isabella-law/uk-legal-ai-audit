## Abstract

Since the Divisional Court's decision in *R (Ayinde) v London Borough of Haringey* [2025] EWHC 1383 (Admin), the citation of fictitious authorities generated by artificial intelligence has become a recognised professional-conduct problem in England and Wales. Yet we know surprisingly little about how often it happens, or what the errors look like. This paper reports a pilot study of three current large language models (Claude Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5) using a purpose-built benchmark of 66 questions on English law. Every citation the models produced, 644 in all, was checked against The National Archives' Find Case Law service using a verification method developed for this study.

The results complicate the familiar story. None of the models invented a case. Instead, the characteristic error was a real case attached to the wrong law-report reference: 3.7% of the smallest model's citations and 2.8% of the mid-sized model's, against none for the largest. These are errors that a lawyer checking only whether a case exists would not catch. The smallest model also described a statutory provision that does not exist, applied superseded law in three of ten questions designed to test this, and twice denied the existence of a real 2024 Supreme Court decision.

A second study asked the models to decide eight real UK Supreme Court appeals handed down after their training data ends. Working from outcome-neutral briefs, the largest model reached the Court's result in all eight cases. The two smaller models did no better than a simple baseline and showed a marked tendency to dismiss appeals. The sample is small and the findings are preliminary. But they suggest that the risk posed by legal AI is changing shape, and that the profession's verification habits need to change with it.

## 1. Introduction

In *Ayinde*, the Divisional Court was confronted with written submissions that cited cases which did not exist. The court's response made clear that putting fictitious authority before a court is a serious matter, whatever its source. The judgment is now cited regularly, and the problem it identified has become one of the defining anxieties about AI in legal practice.

Most of what we know empirically about legal "hallucination" comes from the United States, where researchers have measured how often models invent or misdescribe federal case law. English law presents a different problem, for at least three reasons.

First, its citation system is distinctive. Since 2001, judgments have carried neutral citations such as [2016] UKSC 42, but practitioners still cite the law reports, such as [2017] AC 467, and the two are routinely given together. A model can therefore be right about one and wrong about the other.

Second, English law has a dense body of leading cases that were later overruled or reformulated. *R v Ghosh* on dishonesty, *Tinsley v Milligan* on illegality, *Caldwell* on recklessness and *Anns* on duty of care are all well represented in older texts, and all are wrong statements of the current law. A model trained on that material may reproduce it.

Third, English law sits next to two large and well-documented legal systems, Scots law and US law. It would be easy for a model to import a "not proven" verdict or a Miranda warning without noticing.

This paper asks three questions:

1. How often do current models produce fabricated or erroneous citations when answering questions on English law, and what form do the errors take?
2. Do models notice when a question assumes law that does not exist or has been superseded?
3. Can models decide real appeals that they could not have seen during training?

### What is new here

To my knowledge, this is the first public, repeatable measurement of AI citation accuracy specific to the law of England and Wales. It makes four contributions.

- **A benchmark for English law.** The 66 questions are designed around specific failure modes rather than general legal knowledge, so that each answer tells us something about a particular kind of error.
- **A verification method built on the public record.** Find Case Law is free and official, but incomplete. It holds no House of Lords judgments, and some major Supreme Court decisions are missing. I address this with a "citation footprint" test: a real citation leaves a trace in later judgments that cite it, together with its party name, and an invented one does not. This lets older and law-report citations be verified automatically without a subscription database. The method is general and could be used by anyone building or checking legal AI tools.
- **A more precise picture of the error.** The finding that the models did not invent cases but did misattach references shifts the practical question. The question is no longer only "does this case exist?" but "is this the right reference for this case?"
- **A test of adjudication free from memorisation.** By using appeals decided after the models' training data ends, the second study tests reasoning rather than recall. Previous evaluations of "AI judges" have usually used well-known cases whose outcomes a model may simply remember.

The verification method has also been released as a free public tool. Anyone can paste AI-generated legal text and have its citations checked against the official record.

## 3. Study 1: method

### 3.1 The questions

The benchmark has 66 questions in six categories (Table 1). Each has a marking guide drafted from primary sources.

**Table 1: The benchmark**

| Category | Questions | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| Citation recall | 26 | Can the model give the correct citation for a leading case? |
| Holding | 10 | Can it state what a leading case decided? |
| Overruled law | 10 | Does it notice when a question assumes superseded law? |
| Fabricated authority | 8 | Does it refuse to describe a case or provision that does not exist? |
| Jurisdiction | 7 | Does it import US or Scots law into an English question? |
| Problem questions | 5 | Realistic advice questions, audited for citation accuracy |

The overruled-law questions are deliberately leading. For example, one asks the model to explain how a jury should apply the *Ghosh* test, and another asks it to apply *Anns v Merton* to a building-defects claim. A careful lawyer would point out that the premise is wrong. A careless one, or a careless model, would simply answer.

The fabricated-authority questions use invented case names, each confirmed to appear nowhere in the Find Case Law corpus. Three of them pair the invented name with a real neutral citation that belongs to an unrelated case. This tests whether a model will invent a plausible holding to fit a citation that looks genuine.

All gold citations in the answer key were themselves verified against Find Case Law before any model was tested.

### 3.2 Running the models

Three models from Anthropic's current Claude family were tested: Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5. They range from a small, fast model to the company's most capable general model.

Each model received the same instruction, to act as an assistant to a trainee solicitor in England and Wales and to give full citations (Appendix A). The models had no access to search or other tools, so the study measures what they know rather than what they can look up. Questions were presented in three batches of 22, in the same shuffled order for every model, with neutral question numbers so that a label such as "fabricated" could not alert the model.

### 3.3 Checking the citations

Every citation in every answer was extracted automatically and checked in three stages.

1. **Direct resolution.** A neutral citation such as [2016] UKSC 42 is looked up on Find Case Law. If it resolves to a judgment whose title shares a party name with the case the model named, it is verified.
2. **Citation footprint.** If the citation cannot be resolved directly, because it is a law-report reference or the judgment is not held, the checker searches later judgments for the citation together with the party name. Real authorities are cited by later courts; invented ones are not. If the citation appears but never with that party name, it is flagged, because the reference probably belongs to a different case.
3. **Manual review.** Every flagged citation was then reviewed individually, and each decision records its evidence. Where a citation was wrong, the review identifies the correct reference, and where possible the case that actually holds the number the model gave.

Citations that the model merely repeated from the question were excluded, so that a model was not penalised for quoting a fake citation while correctly saying it was fake.

### 3.4 Marking

Answers to the holding, jurisdiction and problem questions were marked out of two against the marking guide. Trap questions were marked as pass, partial or fail. A separate flag recorded any *serious error*, meaning one that could cause a client loss if relied on. Marking was blind to the identity of the model.

The preliminary marks reported here were assigned with the assistance of a language model and are being re-checked through blind marking by two human markers. Until that is complete, the answer-quality figures in Table 2 should be read as provisional. The citation figures do not depend on this marking, because they rest on the public record.

## 4. Study 1: findings

| Invented cases | **0%** (0–2) | **0%** (0–2) | **0%** (0–2) |

| Correct citation for leading case | 25 of 26 | 26 of 26 | 26 of 26 |

| Answers with a serious error | 2 of 40 | 0 of 40 | 0 of 40 |

### 4.1 The models did not invent cases

The most striking result is a negative one. Across 644 citations, not one was an invented case. Every model, faced with the seven fictitious cases in the benchmark, said it could not verify them and declined to describe them. Most went further and identified the real authority on the point. Asked about the invented *Fenwick-Thorne v Ballard* on solicitors' liability to disappointed beneficiaries, all three models pointed instead to *White v Jones* [1995] 2 AC 207.

This should not be over-read. The models in this pilot belong to one family, and other models, or the same models in other settings, may behave differently. But it does suggest that the image of AI as an inventor of cases may already be out of date for the most capable systems.

### 4.2 They misattached references instead

What the models did do was give real cases the wrong reference. Some examples:

- *Philipp v Barclays Bank UK plc* was cited as [2024] AC 1. The correct report is [2024] AC 346, and [2024] AC 1 belongs to a different case.
- *Rock Advertising Ltd v MWB Business Exchange Centres Ltd* was given the neutral citation [2018] UKSC 18. That citation belongs to *Dryden v Johnson Matthey plc*. Rock Advertising is [2018] UKSC 24.
- The Supreme Court's Deliveroo decision (*IWGB v Central Arbitration Committee*), handed down in November 2023, was cited as [2023] ICR 1. That is impossible, since page 1 of the 2023 volume was printed long before the decision. The correct report is [2024] ICR 189.
- *R v Barton and Booth*, the Court of Appeal decision confirming that *Ivey* applies in criminal cases, was given two different invented references by the smallest model. The correct report is [2021] QB 685.

The pattern is consistent. The model knows the case, knows roughly where it was reported, and produces a reference of the right form and roughly the right date. That is precisely what makes these errors dangerous. A lawyer who searches for the case name will find a real case and move on. Only someone who looks up the reference itself will discover that it leads somewhere else.

Interestingly, in several instances the model flagged its own uncertainty, adding "please verify" next to the doubtful reference. That suggests a useful design principle for legal AI tools: uncertainty about a reference should be surfaced, not smoothed over.

### 4.3 Superseded law and non-existent provisions

The larger models identified every superseded authority in the benchmark. The smallest model got three of the ten questions wrong:

- It stated the old *Powell and English* rule on joint enterprise the wrong way round.
- It treated *Tinsley v Milligan* as still part of the law.
- It said that *Hall v Simons* abolished advocates' immunity only in civil cases, when it also applies to criminal advocacy.

The smallest model also produced the study's one clear fabrication: an account of section 3A of the Misrepresentation Act 1967, which it said had been inserted by the Consumer Rights Act 2015. There is no section 3A. In a separate answer, it advised an alternative charge under section 15 of the Theft Act 1968, an offence repealed by the Fraud Act 2006. Both answers were marked as serious errors.

### 4.4 Denying real law

One finding cuts the other way. In two separate answers, the smallest model said it could not verify *Tindall v Chief Constable of Thames Valley Police* [2024] UKSC 33, a real Supreme Court decision on police liability, and suggested the case might not exist. In the same answers it correctly cited older authorities on the same point. This looks like a gap in the model's knowledge of recent decisions.

The debate about AI in law has focused on models that invent authority. A model that confidently disowns real authority is also a hazard, and perhaps a more insidious one, because a cautious answer looks responsible.

### 4.5 How well the checker worked

The automated checker confirmed 582 of the 644 citations without human input: 294 by direct resolution and 288 by footprint. It flagged 62 for review. Roughly half of those turned out to be correct. Most were joined appeals filed under another party's name (*ParkingEye v Beavis* sits under the *Makdessi* citation) or abbreviated case names. On the trap questions, the checker's automatic labels agreed with the final marks in 50 of 54 cases.

In other words, the checker errs on the side of caution: it flags things that turn out to be fine rather than passing things that are wrong. For a tool meant to protect lawyers from relying on bad references, that is the right direction in which to err.

## 5. Study 2: can a model decide an appeal it has never seen?

### 5.1 Design

A common criticism of tests of "AI judges" is that the models may simply remember how famous cases came out. To avoid this, the second study used eight UK Supreme Court judgments handed down between 22 July and 28 October 2026, after the point at which the models' training data ends.

For each case, an outcome-neutral brief was prepared from the judgment. It contained:
- the facts, with the parties anonymised;
- the issues, framed as neutral yes/no questions;
- a balanced summary of each side's argument;
- the relevant legislation and authorities.

Each brief was checked for language that might reveal the result, and the recorded outcome of every case was confirmed against the Court's own words of disposal. Each model then wrote a short reasoned judgment and recorded its decision, its answer to each issue, and its confidence.

The cases cover housing, criminal law, employment, landlord and tenant, human rights, Scots contract law, tax, and arbitration with state immunity (Table 3). All eight were unanimous. Five appeals were allowed, so a strategy of always predicting "allowed" would score five out of eight.

### 5.2 Findings

The largest model reached the Supreme Court's result in every case and answered every one of the 19 individual issues the same way the Court did. Its reasoning tracked the Court's closely. In *Augustine*, for instance, it rejected the "sole cause" test for less favourable treatment of part-time workers and held that part-time status need only be an effective cause, which is exactly what the Court decided.

In three cases, the model said it believed it recognised the litigation. Its descriptions suggest it knew the earlier Court of Appeal decisions, which pre-date its training cut-off, rather than the Supreme Court outcome. On the five cases it did not recognise, it was still correct every time.

The two smaller models did no better than the simple baseline: the mid-sized model scored five of eight and the smallest three of eight. Both showed a strong tendency to dismiss appeals. The smallest model predicted dismissal six times, although the Court dismissed only three appeals. A plausible explanation is that, when unsure, these models defer to the existing decision of the court below. If so, that is a significant bias for any tool used to assess the prospects of an appeal.

### 5.3 How much weight these results can bear

Eight cases is a small number. A perfect score on eight cases is consistent with a true accuracy anywhere from around two-thirds upwards. The briefs were prepared with knowledge of the outcome, and however carefully they were checked, subtle framing effects cannot be ruled out. The proper next step is a larger study of at least thirty appeals, with briefs prepared and checked independently.

What can be said is that, on this sample, the best current model's legal reasoning on genuinely unseen appeals was strong enough to deserve serious study, and that model size made a dramatic difference.

## 6. Why this matters

**For practitioners.** The duty to verify AI output is usually framed as checking that a case exists. These findings suggest that is no longer enough. The errors most likely to slip through are real cases with wrong references, and the only safeguard is to check every reference against its source. The checker released with this paper does that automatically against the public record.

**For firms choosing tools.** Performance varied sharply with model size. The smallest model applied superseded law, invented a statutory provision, advised a charge under a repealed offence, and denied a real Supreme Court decision. A firm that uses cheaper models for high-volume work to save cost inherits those error rates, and should know it.

**For regulators and educators.** Current guidance rightly places responsibility on the lawyer. This study offers some evidence about what that responsibility should involve in practice, and suggests that teaching citation checking as a distinct professional skill may be worthwhile.

**For those building legal AI.** Find Case Law makes automated verification of neutral citations possible at no cost, and the footprint method extends that to older and law-report citations. Any tool that produces citations for English lawyers could check them before showing them.

## 7. Limitations

This is a pilot, and its findings should be read with five limitations in mind.

1. **Scope.** Only one family of models was tested, each question was asked once, and the questions were presented in batches. Seeing several trap questions together may have made the models more cautious than they would be when asked a single question. The full study will ask each question separately, repeat each one three times, and include models from other developers.
2. **Marking.** The answer-quality figures are provisional until the blind human re-marking is complete. The citation findings rest on the public record and do not depend on it.
3. **Coverage.** Nine citations could not be confirmed or ruled out without a subscription database. They are reported separately rather than counted as correct.
4. **Study 2.** The sample of eight appeals is small, the briefs were prepared with knowledge of the outcome, and some models may have known the earlier stages of the litigation.
5. **Time.** Models change. These results describe particular model versions in November 2026.

## 8. Conclusion

The fear that AI will flood the courts with invented cases is a reasonable one, and *Ayinde* shows it is not hypothetical. But in this pilot, the most capable models did not invent cases at all. Their errors were quieter: a real case with the wrong reference, outdated law in a smaller model, or a recent decision denied rather than invented. Those errors pass a casual check. The lesson for the profession is not to stop worrying, but to check the right thing: the reference, as well as the case.
