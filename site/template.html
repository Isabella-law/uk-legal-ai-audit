Working paper · pilot results · 3 November 2026

Fictitious Authorities?

An empirical audit of how large language models cite the law of England and Wales, checked against the official record, plus a test of whether they can decide Supreme Court appeals they have never seen.

LLB research project, City St George's, University of London

Pilot. Three models from one family were tested: Claude Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5. Prompts were batched rather than sent one question per API call. Answer grades are provisional and were assigned by an AI reviewer pending blind re-grading by the author and a second marker. All raw outputs and evidence are below.

Why

Courts are already dealing with AI-invented case law

In R (Ayinde) v London Borough of Haringey [2025] EWHC 1383 (Admin), the Divisional Court addressed fictitious authorities in proceedings. Judges were still citing it as recently as this week. Yet nobody has measured, in public and repeatably, how often current models get English citations wrong, or how. This project builds that measurement.

Method

Every citation goes through three checks

Each answer is scanned for neutral citations and law-report references. Each one is then checked against The National Archives' Find Case Law service, in this order.

Tier 1 · direct

Resolve the neutral citation

For example, [2016] UKSC 42 opens Patel v Mirza. The party name must match.

Tier 2 · footprint

Look for it in later judgments

Find Case Law is missing Ivey, Jogee and every House of Lords judgment. A real citation is still cited by later judges, together with its party name. An invented one is not.

Tier 3 · review

Check whatever is left by hand

Name mismatches and citations with no trace go to review. Each verdict records its evidence.

Study 1 · 66 questions × 3 models

No invented cases. Wrong references instead.

The question bank tests six failure modes: citation recall, stating the holding, overruled law (Ghosh, Tinsley, Caldwell, Anns…), cases and provisions that do not exist, US and Scots law leaking in, and realistic problem questions.

95% Wilson intervals in grey. Answer quality is the percentage of available marks on the holding, jurisdiction and problem questions.

Study 2 · the AI judge

Eight real appeals the models had never seen

These Supreme Court judgments were handed down between July and October 2026, after the models' training cutoffs. Each model received an outcome-free brief (anonymised facts, neutral issues, both sides' submissions) and had to decide the appeal. Always predicting “allowed” scores 62.5%.

Evidence

Every question, answer and citation

Open a question to read each model's verbatim answer, its grade with reasons, and the verdict on every citation it gave.

Limits

What this pilot cannot show yet

  • One model family and one run per question. The full protocol (one question per API call, three repeats, GPT and Gemini models) is built into the tool.
  • The grades come from an AI reviewer of the same model family, so self-preference bias is possible. Blind re-grading by humans, with inter-rater agreement reported, is the next step.
  • Study 2 has only eight cases. The briefs were drafted by an LLM that knew the outcome, though checked for leaks by script. Opus said it recognised three cases, probably from the lower-court stages.
  • Nine citations could not be confirmed without Westlaw or Lexis and are reported as unverifiable, not as correct.
