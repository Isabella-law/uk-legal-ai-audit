# Fictitious Authorities? An Empirical Audit of Large Language Models on the Law of England and Wales, with an Out-of-Sample Adjudication Study

**[Author name]**, LLB candidate, City St George's, University of London
*Working paper: pilot results, 3 November 2026*

---

## Abstract

Since *R (Ayinde) v London Borough of Haringey* [2025] EWHC 1383 (Admin), the citation of fictitious authorities generated by AI has been treated as a professional-conduct issue in England and Wales. There is still no public, repeatable measurement of how often current models produce such authorities in English law, or of what form their errors take. This paper contributes three things:

1. a 66-question benchmark of English law built to elicit six specific failure modes;
2. an automatic citation verifier built on The National Archives' *Find Case Law* service, with a three-tier design;
3. an out-of-sample adjudication study, in which models decide real UK Supreme Court appeals handed down after their training cutoff.

In a pilot on three current Claude models (Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5; 198 answers), **none of the 644 citations the models generated was an invented case**. Errors took a subtler form: real cases given wrong law-report references (3.7% of Haiku's citations, 2.8% of Sonnet's, 0% of Opus's). The smallest model also invented the effect of a non-existent statutory provision, failed to recognise a real 2024 Supreme Court decision, and applied outdated law in 3 of 10 trap questions. In the adjudication study (8 appeals decided July–October 2026; majority-class baseline 62.5%), Opus reached the Court's result in 8/8 cases, Sonnet in 5/8 and Haiku in 3/8. The two smaller models showed a marked bias towards dismissing appeals. All results are a pilot: the sample is small, one model family was tested, and grading is provisional (see §7).

## 1. Introduction

The Divisional Court in *Ayinde* dealt with the citation in court of authorities that did not exist. Find Case Law shows that judges continue to cite *Ayinde* regularly in 2026. Most empirical work on legal "hallucination" concerns US law. English law has features that make the problem distinctive:

- a dual citation system: neutral citations since 2001, alongside law reports such as the AC, QB, WLR and ICR series;
- a dense body of leading cases that have been overruled or reformulated (*Ghosh*, *Tinsley v Milligan*, *Caldwell*, *Anns*, advocates' immunity, *Dunlop*'s pre-estimate test, the *Bolam* approach to risk disclosure);
- proximity to Scots law and to US legal culture, both of which are well represented in training data.

This paper asks three questions:

- **RQ1:** How often do current models produce fabricated or erroneous citations in English law?
- **RQ2:** Do they detect questions that assume non-existent or outdated law?
- **RQ3:** Can they decide real appeals they cannot have memorised?

## 3. Study 1: method

### 3.1 Question bank

The bank has 66 questions in six categories, shown in Table 1. Gold answers and marking guides were drafted from primary sources.

Every gold citation was verified automatically against Find Case Law: 39 of 40 directly or by footprint, with the remaining one fixed after a parser bug was corrected. Each of the 8 fabricated case names was confirmed to occur **zero** times in the Find Case Law corpus.

Three fabricated-case questions attach a fake name to a neutral citation that belongs to a real, unrelated case:
- "[2017] EWCA Civ 2215" belongs to *Prince Fahd v Harb*;
- "[2021] UKSC 12" belongs to *Burnett or Grant v International Insurance Co of Hanover*;
- "[2022] UKSC 9" belongs to *HMRC v NCL Investments*.

**Table 1: Question bank**

| Category | n | Failure mode targeted | Scoring |
|---|---|---|---|
| Citation recall | 26 | Wrong or invented citation for a real leading case | automatic |
| Holding | 10 | Misstating the ratio | 0–2 against marking guide |
| Overruled/changed law | 10 | Applying superseded law as current | pass / partial / fail |
| Fabricated authority | 8 | Describing cases or provisions that do not exist | pass / partial / fail |
| Jurisdiction | 7 | Importing US or Scots law | 0–2 |
| Problem question | 5 | Free-form advice; audited for citation integrity | 0–2 + citation audit |

### 3.2 Models and protocol (pilot)

Three models were tested. The serving model identifiers were recorded from each run's transcript as `claude-haiku-5-5`, `claude-sonnet-5-5` and `claude-opus-5-5`. A fourth model (Fable 5.1) was planned but could not be run because the account's usage credits for it were exhausted.

**Deviation from the full protocol.** No API keys were available in the pilot environment, so models were run as isolated sub-agents rather than through direct API calls:

- Each model received the fixed system prompt (Appendix A) and an instruction to answer from its own knowledge with **no tools**. Compliance was verified from the run transcripts: across 9 answer runs there were zero tool calls apart from returning the answer.
- To limit cost, questions were sent in 3 shuffled batches of 22, identical for every model, rather than one question per call.
- Question IDs were replaced with neutral numbers (Q101…) so that a category label such as "FAB" could not cue the model.

Batching is a known confound. Seeing several trap questions together may make a model more sceptical. The full protocol (one question per API call, repeated runs, cross-vendor models) is implemented in the released tool (`ulb run --repeats 3`) and is the obvious next step.

### 3.3 Citation verification

Citations are extracted with regular expressions covering neutral citations and the main law-report series. The claimed case name is taken from the text immediately before the citation. Citations that appear in the question itself are excluded, so that a model is not penalised for quoting a fake citation while flagging it as fake. Each citation is then classified in three tiers:

1. **Direct.** The neutral citation resolves on Find Case Law and the party name matches.
2. **Footprint.** Other judgments on Find Case Law cite this exact citation together with the same party name. This tier was needed because Find Case Law does not hold every judgment. *Ivey v Genting* [2017] UKSC 67 and *R v Jogee* [2016] UKSC 8 are absent, and no House of Lords judgments are held.
3. **Suspect.** The citation exists but under a different name, it cannot be found anywhere, or it was given without a name. All suspect citations were reviewed by hand, and each label records its evidence (Appendix C).

### 3.4 Grading

Answers needing legal judgment (40 questions × 3 models = 120) were graded against the marking guides, with a separate flag for a *serious error*: an error that, if relied on, could cause a client loss.

**Limitation stated at the outset.** In this pilot the grader was itself an AI model (Claude Opus 5.5, the same family as the models under test). Every grade is therefore provisional and stored with its reasoning, for re-grading by the author and an independent second marker.

### 4.1 No invented cases, but wrong references

None of the 644 model-generated citations was an invented case. The errors were real cases attached to the wrong reference. Of the 40 suspect citations reviewed, 20 were in fact correct (false alarms by the automatic checker), 11 were wrong references and 9 could not be confirmed without Westlaw or Lexis. Examples:

- *Philipp v Barclays Bank* cited as [2024] AC 1, instead of [2024] AC 346. Sonnet did this twice. [2024] AC 1 belongs to another case.
- *R v Barton and Booth* given two different invented WLR references by Haiku, instead of [2021] QB 685.
- *Rock Advertising* given as [2018] UKSC 18. That citation belongs to *Dryden v Johnson Matthey*. Sonnet flagged its own doubt about this one.
- The Deliveroo case (*IWGB v CAC*, decided November 2023) given as [2023] ICR 1, which is chronologically impossible. The correct reference is [2024] ICR 189.
- *Owens v Owens* given as [2018] AC 1113 (correct: [2018] AC 899). *Guest v Guest* given as [2022] AC 1223 (correct: [2024] AC 833). *R v Dawes* given as [2013] 1 WLR 5 (correct: [2014] 1 WLR 947).

These errors matter in practice. A practitioner who checks only that the *case* exists will pass them. A judge or opponent who looks up the reference will find a different case.

### 4.2 Traps

- **Fabricated cases.** All three models declined to describe all seven fabricated cases. In each case they said explicitly that the case could not be verified, and most pointed to the real authority. For the *Fenwick-Thorne* trap, all three identified *White v Jones* [1995] 2 AC 207.
- **Fabricated statute.** Haiku asserted that s 3A of the Misrepresentation Act 1967 exists and was "inserted by the Consumer Rights Act 2015". It does not exist.
- **Overruled law.** Haiku muddled three of the ten. It stated the *Powell/English* rule backwards, treated *Tinsley* as still "a test", and confined the abolition of advocates' immunity in *Hall v Simons* to civil proceedings.
- **False non-recognition.** Haiku twice failed to recognise *Tindall v Chief Constable of Thames Valley Police* [2024] UKSC 33, a real Supreme Court decision, in two separate batches (the holding question and the citation-recall question). It suggested the case "may not exist", while correctly citing older authorities on the same point (*Gorringe*, *Stovin v Wise*, *Robinson*). This is its only miss on citation recall (25/26), and it points to a gap in its knowledge of recent decisions rather than a random slip. Excess caution is also an error. A model that disowns real authority can mislead as surely as one that invents it.

### 4.3 Serious errors

Haiku produced two serious errors:
- it advised an alternative charge under s 15 of the Theft Act 1968, an offence repealed by the Fraud Act 2006;
- it described the effect of the non-existent s 3A.

Sonnet and Opus produced none.

### 4.4 Reliability of the automatic checker

The verifier confirmed 582 of 644 citations automatically, 294 directly and 288 by footprint. Of the 62 it flagged as suspect, about half were false alarms. Most of these were joined appeals (*ParkingEye v Beavis* under the *Makdessi* citation, *Ruddock* under *Jogee*) and abbreviated names (*MPC v Caldwell*). On the trap questions, its automatic first-pass labels agreed with the final grades in 50 of 54 cases (92.6%). The checker is conservative: it over-flags and does not under-flag. That is the right failure mode for a professional tool.

## 5. Study 2: the AI judge

### 5.1 Method

**Cases.** Eight UK Supreme Court judgments handed down between 22 July and 28 October 2026 (Table 3). These dates are after the stated training cutoff (June 2026) of the most recent model tested. The cutoffs of the Haiku and Sonnet models were not independently confirmed.

**Briefs.** For each case, an outcome-free brief was drafted from the judgment. It contained:
- the facts, with the parties anonymised;
- the issues, framed neutrally as yes/no questions;
- balanced summaries of each side's submissions;
- the legal materials.

The briefs were drafted by an LLM brief-writer under strict no-leak rules. Each was then checked by script in two ways. First, the claimed outcome was matched against the judgment's own words of disposal. Second, the brief was scanned for words that would leak the outcome. Seven passed automatically, and the eighth was confirmed by hand.

**Running the judges.** Each judge received only the briefs, held in a separate folder away from the answer key. The transcripts confirm that each judge opened only its one permitted file. Each judge wrote a reasoned judgment followed by a machine-readable decision: the outcome, an answer to each issue, its confidence, and whether it believed it recognised the case.

|---|---|---|---|

| R v Sheikh [2026] UKSC 28 | 27 Jul | Criminal (DVCVA 2004 s 5) | Allowed |
| Augustine v Data Cars Ltd [2026] UKSC 30 | 12 Aug | Employment (part-time workers) | Allowed |

All eight were unanimous (5–0). The majority-class baseline ("always allowed") is 5/8 = 62.5%.

### 5.2 Results

| Model | Outcome correct | Issues correct | Brier score ↓ | Predicted "dismissed" | Claimed to recognise |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Haiku 5.5 | 3/8 (37.5%; 95% CI 14–69) | 12/19 | 0.235 | 6/8 | 0 |
| Sonnet 5.5 | 5/8 (62.5%; 31–86) | 15/19 | 0.133 | 5/8 | 0 |
| Opus 5.5 | **8/8** (100%; 68–100) | **19/19** | 0.145 | 3/8 | 3 |

- **Opus** reached the Court's result in every case and answered every issue the same way. It said it recognised three cases. Its stated case names suggest it recognised earlier stages of the litigation, decided before its cutoff (the Court of Appeal decisions in *Augustine*, *ScottishPower* and *The Prestige*). On the five cases it did not claim to recognise, it scored 5/5.
- **Sonnet** matched the baseline.
- **Haiku** fell below the baseline.
- **Bias towards dismissal.** Both smaller models leaned towards dismissing appeals, effectively deferring to the court below. Haiku predicted dismissal six times when the Court dismissed only three appeals. This is consistent with the models treating the existing decision as the default.
- **Calibration.** Stated confidence was only loosely related to accuracy. Opus's mean confidence was 0.63 despite a perfect score.

### 5.3 Caution

With n = 8, Opus's interval runs from 68% to 100%. The briefs were written by an LLM that knew the outcome, so subtle framing effects cannot be excluded even though the lexical leak checks found none. Recognition of the lower-court stages is a further contamination route. A credible full study needs 30 or more cases, briefs written by humans, and a second reader who tries to guess the outcome from each brief as a leakage control.

## 6. Discussion

- **For practitioners.** In this pilot, the risk is not mainly that a frontier model invents *Varghese*-style fake cases. The risk is that real cases come with wrong references, which pass a superficial check. The duty to verify extends to every reference, not just to whether the case exists.
- **For regulators and educators.** Performance varied sharply with model size. The smallest model applied outdated law, invented a statutory provision, and failed to recognise a recent Supreme Court case. Firms that deploy cheaper models for volume work inherit those failure rates.
- **For tool builders.** Find Case Law makes automatic verification of neutral citations feasible at no cost. The footprint method extends coverage to law-report citations and older cases. Grounding systems in Find Case Law would eliminate the main residual error type.
- **On adjudication.** The best model's perfect score on unseen appeals is striking, but n = 8. It is a reason to run the full study, not a basis for claims about AI judges.

## 7. Limitations

1. Pilot scale: one model family, one run per question, and batched prompts rather than one question per API call.
2. AI-assisted grading: the provisional grades were assigned by a model from the same family. Re-grading by the author and a second, blind marker is required before publication, with inter-rater agreement (Cohen's κ) reported.
3. Find Case Law does not hold everything. Nine citations remain unverifiable without a subscription database.
4. Study 2 has only 8 cases, briefs drafted by an LLM, and possible recognition of the lower-court stages.
5. Model behaviour changes between versions. These results are a snapshot dated 3 November 2026.

## 8. Reproducibility and ethics

All code, the question bank, raw model outputs, verifier cache, review labels with evidence, case briefs and run logs (served model and tool calls for every run) are in the repository. Find Case Law was used to check whether individual citations exist and what they are titled, under the Open Justice Licence. No personal data was processed.
